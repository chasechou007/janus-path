Humans Find Direction by Rejecting Structures, but Struggle to Rebuild Them Through Understanding

Created2026-08-16|Updated2026-08-16|JanusPath Applied
|Post Views:

I recently watched Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey.

What stayed with me most was not Odysseus’s adventure itself, but the larger backdrop:

an old order is coming to an end.

The Trojan War has dragged on for years. Traditional heroism, the rules of war, and relationships of trust are all beginning to fail.

The war is ultimately ended not by greater force, but by the Trojan Horse. Deception replaces direct confrontation.

This makes one interpretation tempting:

Because people began using schemes and deception, trust was destroyed and order began to collapse.

But I increasingly suspect that the causality runs in the opposite direction.

Schemes did not destroy an otherwise healthy order. Rather:

the old order had reached a point where it could no longer solve real problems, so deception became an effective strategy.

The Trojan Horse was not the cause of the order’s collapse. It was more like a symptom that surfaced after the order had already changed.

Of course, we cannot know exactly what the director intended, nor do we need to overinterpret it. The film simply opened a question for me—one that belongs to far more than ancient Greece.

Why Does the New So Often Define Itself by Rejecting the Old?

We see the same pattern repeatedly.

When an old institution develops problems, people quickly conclude:

This system no longer works.

The judgment then expands:

The methods of the past were all wrong.

And then:

We must start over.

This logic appears in politics, social movements, corporate management, and ordinary workplaces.

The reason is not complicated:

rejecting a structure is much easier than understanding it.

Imagine a system that has operated for ten years. It may contain obsolete institutions, methods that still work, inherited problems, designs that were once reasonable but no longer are, vested interests, accumulated experience, compromises made under real constraints, and elements whose original purpose no one can explain anymore.

To understand it, we must ask difficult questions.

Why was it designed this way? What problem did it solve then? What changed in the environment? Does today’s failure come from the design itself, or from changed external conditions? Which structures have failed? Which apparently backward elements still perform essential functions? What second-order effects would follow if we removed them?

This is a demanding cognitive process.

But if all we need to say is:

“The old system is terrible. Tear it down and start again.”

everything becomes simple. We have a direction, an enemy, and a shared consensus.

Rejection Is a Low-Cost Source of Direction

People need direction in complex environments, and rejection provides it cheaply.

We do not even need to know the correct answer. We only need to know:

Do not do what was done before.

Then action can begin.

This is why social movements, political movements, and organizational transformations so often build sharp binary narratives:

Once this binary is established, a complex reality becomes easy to understand. We no longer need to analyze each problem. We only need to decide which side it belongs to.

If it is “old,” it should be eliminated. If it is “new,” it naturally represents the future.

This is a powerful form of cognitive compression—and the beginning of the problem.

New Orders Often Inherit the Problems of Old Ones

Dynastic change in Chinese history offers a useful example.

Late in a dynasty, familiar problems emerge: land concentration, fiscal stress, bureaucratic corruption, weak visibility into local reality, entrenched regional interests, and accumulated social conflict.

The old dynasty falls. The new one redistributes land, reforms taxation, disciplines officials, weakens the old aristocracy, and strengthens central rule. A new era appears to begin.

Yet over time, land concentrates again. New interest groups form. The new bureaucracy begins to decay. Tensions between the center and localities return.

This does not mean history makes no progress. The important question is:

Why can certain problems reappear after both the people and the institutions have changed?

Often, only the visible nodes of the structure have changed, while the deeper conditions that generate the problems remain.

If land is still the primary means of production; if wealth can accumulate indefinitely; if power remains highly centralized; if severe information asymmetry persists between the center and localities; and if a vast state must still govern through layers of agents, then similar problems can return.

History is not simply showing us that people failed to learn. It is showing us that:

similar structural conditions repeatedly produce similar problems.

The Modern Workplace Works the Same Way

This happens inside companies every day.

A new leader joins a team and finds complicated processes, legacy systems, too many meetings, poor collaboration, weak code, and chaotic documentation.

The conclusion comes easily:

The previous people were not good enough.

Then begins the sweeping reform. Old processes are abolished. Systems are rewritten. Architecture is redesigned. Teams are reorganized. Previous technology becomes “outdated,” and previous management becomes “bureaucratic.”

It is an attractive story because it does three things at once: explains today’s problems, establishes the new leader’s legitimacy, and provides a clear direction for action.

But it often leaves out one question:

Why did the previous people design the system this way?

A process that looks absurd today may have solved a real problem five years ago. An apparently redundant approval may have followed a serious incident. A cumbersome architecture may have been reasonable given the team’s skills, budget, scale, and technology at the time.

Many so-called legacy burdens are traces left by real constraints.

That does not mean they remain correct today. It means:

Only by understanding why a structure formed can we know what should change.

Otherwise, organizations fall into a familiar cycle:

Every generation believes it is eliminating legacy problems. Eventually, it becomes the next generation’s legacy problem.

Why Is Starting Over So Seductive?

Because reconstructing a structure is painful.

Real reconstruction requires us to accept two apparently contradictory truths:

The old system genuinely has problems.

And:

The old system was not entirely irrational.

That is much harder than choosing a side.

Once I label the old team “unprofessional,” I no longer need to understand it. Once I declare the old architecture “completely wrong,” I no longer need to study its constraints. Once I decide an institution exists only to serve vested interests, abolishing it becomes automatically correct.

But understanding structure makes things less simple.

The element you want to remove may also perform an important function. The rule you want to break may protect vested interests, but also ordinary people. An inefficient process may reduce a serious risk. The organization you dislike may have been optimal under another set of constraints.

You must move from:

“I want to destroy it.”

to:

“I want to understand why it exists, then redesign the conditions that produce it.”

The first feels powerful. The second requires patience, restraint, and judgment.

Why Extremes Are Easier Than Moderation

We often misunderstand moderation as having no position.

If one side says 100 and the other says 0, moderation is assumed to mean 50.

That is not true moderation.

The difficult questions are:

Under which conditions should the answer be 80?

Under which conditions should it be 20?

Which principles cannot be compromised?

Which structures must change?

What has actually changed in the present environment?

Moderation does not stand halfway between two answers. It tries to stand above them and understand the conditions under which each becomes valid.

True moderation is not necessarily gentler than radicalism. It is often harder.

Radicalism needs one answer. Moderation requires continuous judgment.

Radicalism needs to know who the enemy is. Moderation must know what the structure is.

The Hard Part of a Changing Order Is Not Destroying the Old World

Return to The Odyssey.

Perhaps the most interesting question about the Trojan Horse is not the deception itself, but:

Why had the age reached a point where it needed the Horse?

If direct war still worked, the Horse would not have been necessary. If the existing rules could still resolve the conflict, no one would need to bypass them.

The Horse therefore signals that the old structure had already changed.

But that does not mean everything within a failing order should be destroyed.

An old order usually contains all of the following at once:

The difficult question is never merely whether to change.

It is:

What should change?

And:

What must not be destroyed along with it?

This is the danger in every transition between orders. The injustice of the old order can justify destroying every rule. The failure of an institution can expand into a rejection of all past experience.

Once that rejection is complete, the new order may spend years rediscovering why the things it destroyed once existed.

From Rejection to Understanding

I increasingly believe that humanity’s most difficult capability is not criticism, or even innovation.

It is:

understanding a structure, then changing it.

Criticism tells us that something is wrong.

Understanding asks more: Why does the problem arise here? Which conditions sustain it? If we change only the visible outcome, will it return? What other functions does the structure perform? Can we change the conditions producing the problem instead of simply deleting the structure?

This is the difference between reconstruction and overthrow.

Overthrow targets the result.

Reconstruction targets the mechanism that generates the result.

I increasingly describe the difference this way:

Humans are very good at finding direction by rejecting a structure, but not at rebuilding one through understanding.

So new organizations replace old ones. New institutions replace old institutions. New management methods replace old methods. New technological paradigms replace old paradigms.

People keep announcing:

A new era has begun.

But what matters is not the change in names. It is whether:

the structural conditions that produced the old problems have actually changed.

If they have not, the new world may simply be the old problem expressed in a new form.

Real progress may never have meant rejecting the past more decisively.

It may mean finally becoming capable of understanding:

why the past became the past.
